Expanded to include a full brass section, the band combine joyful melodic pop, cinematic production and avant-garde experimentation, moving freely between orchestral pop, classical minimalism and post-punk. Their latest releases, the Valediction EP and album Love in the Misanthropocene, explore vulnerability, hope and love amid contemporary political and ecological precarity. Guided by their manifesto, Maximum Irony! Maximum Sincerity, Grubby Mitts balance playful absurdity with genuine emotional force. Their live performances bring music, video and visual art together in what feels like an exhibition in concert form, building through shifting rhythms and emotional registers towards crescendos of cacophonous yelping. They have performed at the ICA, Hayward Gallery, Whitechapel Gallery, Latitude and Austria's Donaufestival, and toured UK artist-run spaces. It is a song of mourning, of letting go of someone, and the hope attached to that person. But as the song unfolds, it builds on a different idea: as we abandon hope, we adopt an ‘infinite resignation’, which can, only in the moment of giving up, create a faith in the future. Pessimism may be a better coping mechanism. Where hope is complacent, pessimism requires action. The first lines of the track are from frontman Andy Holden’s diary, but as the song unfolds there are many quotations: Kafka, T.S. Eliot, Julian of Norwich and Hilary Mantel. The choir of voices turns the specificity into universal, incorporating personal perspective but also ecological, planetary. Then there is the joyful resignation of a house beat, the thud of a heartbeat, and an invitation to dance. It’s Holden’s attempt at a dance anthem: designed to be played in Ibiza, while the world cooks. The song thrush joins in, as does Holden, knocking a saucepan and wood blocks together, as the choir, in their insistence, gradually shifts the emphasis from the second part of the refrain back to the
